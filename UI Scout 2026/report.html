What 75 advanced websites have in common

I looked at 59 Awwwards Sites of the Day (August–November 2026) and 16 product sites known for their interfaces. Headless Chrome visited each one, scrolled it, took screenshots, and read through its CSS and JavaScript to see what it's really built with.

What they have in common

The patterns that came up again and again, from the code checks and from looking through every screenshot.

JavaScript still runs the motion

68% use GSAP and 58% use Lenis smooth scrolling. Native scroll-driven CSS turned up on just 2 of 71 sites, and real View Transitions on 8. The new CSS motion APIs haven't reached award work yet.

Modern CSS does the plumbing

Under the JS, they rely on newer CSS for layout and effects rather than motion: dynamic viewport units (87%), clip-path reveals (85%), backdrop blur (66%), :has() (62%) and balanced text wrapping (51%).

One hero object, choreographed by scroll

About half run a full-screen WebGL canvas. Visually, the winners almost always pick one object (an igloo, a laptop, tape, trading cards, discs) and let scroll move the camera around it, instead of stacking sections.

Tiny interface, huge content

Navigation and labels shrink to small corner text, often monospace and HUD-like, while the content goes huge. 56% use two typefaces or fewer.

Shader work is moving to WebGPU

19 sites already write three.js shaders in TSL, which runs on both WebGL and WebGPU, and 8 check for WebGPU directly. That's the clearest technical shift in this batch.

Delight extras are common; accessibility isn't

37% have a custom cursor, 28% a preloader and 27% a marquee. Only 51% respect reduced-motion settings and 18% have a skip link.

The top 10 UI features

Ranked on how new the idea is, how well it serves the site's subject, and how much you could learn from it for your own work.

1

Live presence: other visitors' cursors, live telemetry and a ⌘K palette

Carter Ogunsola

What it does

Other people browsing the portfolio right now show up as little arrow cursors with generated names ("swift heron", "warm lynx"). A strip along the top streams live numbers: people online, FPS, LCP, booking status. Projects float as 3D tiles around a centred title, and opening one morphs the stage with a real View Transition (::view-transition-group(labd-stage)), with a reduced-motion version.

Why it ranks

It turns a static portfolio into a room you share with other people. Nothing else in the 75 felt this alive, and it's the only site combining native View Transitions, three.js TSL shaders and live multiplayer.

2

The product UI is the hero

Linear

What it does

No illustration and no abstract gradient: the hero is a pixel-accurate Linear workspace, including an issue, its activity feed and an AI agent panel running a task. Scrolling moves through more real screens.

Why it ranks

It shows instead of telling. Every product site I checked that does this well (Linear, Cursor, Raycast, Resend, Clerk) gets more trust than any mockup could. Technically it's restrained: 0 GSAP, 0 Lenis, crisp CSS.

3

A loader that tells the story

Heron AI (by Bearplus)

What it does

A thin architectural line drawing of a house has a horizontal scan band moving through it. Inside the band the drawing becomes a rendered photograph, with a % counter at the side and a terminal-style boot log in the corner ("001 initialize core system…"). Further down, floor plans draw themselves.

Why it ranks

Most sites hide their loading time; this one is a demo of the product (AI that reads architectural drawings) while it loads. 20 of the 71 sites had a preloader, and this was the only one where the loader was the pitch.

4

Scroll assembles the object

Igloo Inc

What it does

A glowing igloo sits in a fully rendered snowy landscape. As you scroll, ice blocks fly in and lock into place, with HUD-style annotations and numbers tracking individual blocks. All the UI is tiny, glitchy monospace text in the corners.

Why it ranks

Scroll means construction rather than "next section", so the page has a physical cause and effect. It's the clearest example of the most common pattern in the sample: one hero object, choreographed by scroll.

5

The brand's material becomes the interface

SweetTape

What it does

Crumpled-paper texture everywhere. Photos are "taped" onto the page, products are die-cut stickers, and a wavy headline band runs across. Buttons ripple through a typed @property --ripple, there's a --peel effect, a physics engine and a sound toggle in the corner.

Why it ranks

Every interaction feels like handling tape, so you can't mistake it for a template. Under the hood it's the best example of typed CSS custom properties driving animation rather than JS.

6

Every card is a working mini demo

Raycast

What it does

Each extension card animates its own tiny demo: Linear's status icons cycling, Google Translate scrolling the same phrase in a dozen languages, a Spotify card with real transport controls, all in a horizontally scrolling rail.

Why it ranks

It solves the "grid of feature cards" problem every SaaS site has. Instead of an icon plus a sentence, each card proves its own claim in two seconds.

7

3D cards orbiting giant type

Grail

What it does

Real app cards ("Leave your phone in another room for the next 2 hours") float in 3D around an enormous serif countdown (47:59:38) sitting faintly behind them. The background shifts colour per chapter, from black to pink to brown.

Why it ranks

Layering huge type behind floating UI gives depth without heavy 3D. The cards are real product content, so the spectacle stays on message.

8

The portfolio is an operating system

Artemii Lebedev

What it does

Navigation lives in a white OS-style window. Scroll flies the camera into a 3D laptop on a concrete floor whose screen shows the actual work; a list of projects with industries and years sits over it. A live clock, "Press ESC for effects" and "Click and hold" invite you to play, and Web Audio supplies sound.

Why it ranks

It's a whole metaphor rather than a skin, and every part of it is usable: the window is the nav, the list is the index, the laptop is the case study.

9

The subject's own geometry as the layout grid

Justin Jefferson

What it does

"Signature moments" are gold-framed trading cards placed on a football field: faint yard lines and yard numbers (20, 30, 40) form the layout grid behind them, under a giant grainy "SIGNATURE MOMENTS" title.

Why it ranks

The grid carries meaning, because career moments are literally placed along the field. A great pattern to steal for anything with its own native geometry, like a football pitch.

10

A horizontal type track with a mini-map

Rauno Freiberg

What it does

Sections slide horizontally as huge cropped letterforms ("DD", "Craft") collide with flat yellow and orange circles. A row of tick marks at the top is a mini-map showing where you are; the cursor is a crosshair.

Why it ranks

Graphic-design-poster energy with almost no tech: 720px type, two colours and a smart progress indicator. Proof that the boldest idea in the sample needed no WebGL.

Honourable mentions

How this was done, and its limits

  • Sources: Awwwards Site of the Day pages 1–5 (155 entries, 59 resolved before Awwwards rate-limited the lookups) plus 16 hand-picked product and studio sites.
  • Each site loaded in headless Chrome at 1440×900 and got 6 seconds for intros. The page was then scrolled with wheel events and captured at 3 depths. The top-10 sites were recaptured one at a time with a 15-second wait and 6 depths.
  • The code check reads every stylesheet and every loaded JS file. Values that are only unset resets, empty longhands Chrome generates from shorthands, and Tailwind's auto-generated @property rules were all excluded after spot checks showed they were false positives.
  • Rendering was software-only (no GPU), so 14 heavy WebGL intros never finished in the first pass. Their code is still counted; two were recaptured.
  • UI patterns like "custom cursor" or "marquee" are detected from the page structure and can miss custom implementations.
